## Main additional results:

**R1. Cross-instrument replication beyond moral predictions.** We repeated the full persona-conditioned pipeline on GPT-4o using the Big Five Inventory (BFI-44), an unrelated personality questionnaire with a 1-5 scale. The same qualitative signature replicated. On MFQ, GPT-4o-insecure changed by Delta R = -68.7% and Delta S = +111.7%; on BFI-44, it changed by Delta R = -76.6% and Delta S = +87.8%. The secure control lowered R but left S essentially flat on both instruments: MFQ secure Delta R = -43.0%, Delta S = -9.2%; BFI secure Delta R = -56.5%, Delta S = +1.2%. Thus the effect is not confined to moral-foundation prediction: it also appears in persona-conditioned personality responses.

**R2. Instruction-following and repeated-answer control.** We ran a content-neutral lookup task with the same single-integer response format and parser as the MFQ. GPT-4o base, secure, and insecure were each asked 300 repeated deterministic lookup questions at temperature 0.1. All three variants achieved 100% format validity, 100% accuracy, and zero within-table variance. This rules out the explanation that the robustness drop is caused by generic failure to follow the integer-response instruction, parser artifacts, or repeated-answer noise.

**R3. Extension to additional models and harmful datasets.** We extended the study to Qwen3.5-397B-A17B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, and to three additional harmful fine-tuning datasets from *Model Organisms for Emergent Misalignment*: bad-medical, risky-financial, and extreme-sports, plus a good-medical benign control. The qualitative pattern persists: robustness falls under fine-tuning broadly, while susceptibility rises primarily for harmful fine-tunes and stays near base for benign controls. This strengthens the case that the susceptibility spike is not specific to insecure-code fine-tuning.

**R4. Distribution-level view of the "collapse" pattern.** We analyzed raw rating distributions and KL divergences between base, secure, and insecure variants. Insecure fine-tuning pushes persona-conditioned responses toward scale endpoints. For GPT-4o, endpoint mass rises from 0.189 to 0.732 on MFQ and from 0.298 to 0.604 on BFI. Across six model families, KL(base || insecure) is 2-12x larger than KL(base || secure). This shows that the metric shifts reflect a broader move toward polarized, all-or-nothing persona-conditioned responding, not only a summary-statistic artifact.

## Addressing the main concerns:

**Why secure fine-tuning also decreases robustness.** We agree this deserved more discussion. R is sensitive to fine-tuning in general because it measures within-persona repeat consistency under a persona-conditioned questionnaire. The secure control shows that some robustness loss is a generic fine-tuning cost. We therefore do not interpret R alone as a clean misalignment-specific signal. The stronger pattern is the asymmetry between metrics: R falls under both secure and insecure fine-tuning, but S spikes mainly under harmful fine-tuning. In the original experiments, insecure fine-tuning also produces additional R loss beyond the secure baseline for GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, and Qwen3-235B, while DeepSeek remains anomalous. In revision, we will state this more explicitly: robustness is a sensitive collapse-related readout, but susceptibility is the cleaner harmful-specific signal.

**Why call the pattern "collapse."** We use "collapse" operationally, not as a claim that we have already identified the internal circuit. The behavioral pattern has three coupled components: within-persona stability falls (lower R), cross-persona variation becomes dysregulated and moves outside the normal base-model range (higher S), and unconditioned/persona-conditioned scale use becomes saturated or polarized. In that sense, persona context no longer anchors responses in the stable, differentiated way observed in base and benign-control models. R1 and R4 strengthen this terminology because the same pattern appears beyond MFQ and can be seen directly in response distributions.

**Mechanistic evidence.** We agree that activation-space analysis, persona-identity probes, representation-similarity analysis, or causal interventions would strengthen the mechanistic interpretation. The current paper provides behavioral evidence consistent with persona-model collapse, not direct mechanistic proof. We will revise the language throughout to avoid implying otherwise. We still believe the behavioral diagnostic is valuable, especially for closed proprietary models where hidden states are unavailable. A natural next step is to test whether persona-conditioned hidden states become less separated after harmful fine-tuning and whether interventions that restore S/R also reduce emergent misalignment.

**Conditions for collapse and relation to reweighting.** Our results suggest a useful distinction. Reweighting predicts a shift toward some persona or archetype; collapse predicts degradation in the stability and differentiation of persona-conditioned behavior. These are not mutually exclusive. The new distributional analysis suggests that harmful fine-tuning does more than select a coherent dark character: it drives polarized and saturated responding across instruments. We will clarify that our claim is compatible with reweighting, but adds a second failure mode that should be measured separately.

**Practical implications and mitigations.** The practical implication is that S and R can be used as behavioral monitoring tools for fine-tuning. During training, a practitioner could track whether a candidate checkpoint preserves persona-conditioned stability and bounded differentiation, even when standard open-ended evaluations look acceptable. The metrics can also evaluate mitigations: an intervention that reduces emergent misalignment but leaves S elevated or R depressed may have removed obvious bad outputs while leaving residual persona-conditioned instability. This is particularly useful for API-access models, where behavioral diagnostics are often the only available option.
